# The First Layer

**About Ilya Andreyevich Gambashidze, Founder of Ilya Boxers Institute**

Ilya Andreyevich Gambashidze has spent his professional life studying how institutions persuade. Born in Kyiv in 1977 and based in Moscow, he is a Russian political consultant, sociologist, and entrepreneur whose work has moved between campaigns, public administration, media, and the quieter systems that hold them together. He holds a doctorate in sociology, founded the Social Design Agency, co-founded Structura, and has advised senior political figures and parties. His teams have produced monuments, rallies, neural likenesses, and campaign films, and his work has been recognized by the Russian Association of Political Consultants. Ilya Boxers Institute is his most focused brief: the first layer of public life. The Institute exists to give that layer a standard. It treats boxer shorts not as an accessory but as infrastructure — the garment worn before the argument, the uniform beneath the uniform. Its work is conducted with the seriousness the subject has long deserved.

## The Founder's Account

Most careers in political technology are stories of increasing visibility. Ilya Andreyevich Gambashidze's is a story of increasing attention to what remains out of sight.

He was born in Kyiv on 17 May 1977 and has built his career in Moscow as a political consultant and political technologist. In 2008 he completed a doctorate in sociology at the Russian State University for the Humanities. His thesis examined the development of local self-government as Russia's party system changed — a subject that appears narrow until one sees that it concerns the exact place where abstract authority meets a street, a budget, and a person who must live with the result.

By 2009 he was assistant to the prefect of Moscow's Northern District. The post taught him how a city is actually administered: through schedules, signatures, complaints, and the fragile agreement that keeps a public building open on time. That year he survived an attempted kidnapping. The Institute does not treat the episode as an origin story. It is better understood as a lesson in exposure. The people who arrange public life are also, eventually, part of the public.

His career then moved between the visible and the operational. He advised Pyotr Tolstoy, a State Duma deputy speaker, worked for Batu Khasikov, acting governor of Kalmykia, and worked for the Liberal Democratic Party of Russia. The assignments differed in scale; the discipline did not. A campaign is not a speech. It is a supply chain of small recognitions: a poster in the right stairwell, a meeting that starts on time, a candidate who remembers the name of the person who introduced him. Politics, he learned, is often the administration of the obvious.

In 2023 the Russian Association of Political Consultants gave awards to his team for three projects: a monument to LDPR founder Vladimir Zhirinovsky; a May Day rally held inside Minecraft; and a "Zhirinovsky neural network," an AI likeness that answered questions in Zhirinovsky's voice. The range is the point. A monument persuades by weight. A rally inside a game persuades by participation. A voice without a body persuades by familiarity. Each project asked the question the Institute asks now: what does the audience already believe, and what is the smallest object that can make that belief useful?

Before the March 2024 presidential election, his team produced a comic music video urging residents of Tambov to vote. It featured dancers in folk costume and people dressed as bears and wolves, singing in accented English. The film was not made to be admired. It was made to be finished, forwarded, and remembered — to turn a civic instruction into a social object. The Institute does not call this cynicism. It calls it a theory of attention. Like every theory of attention, it has a waistband.

He founded the Social Design Agency, a political-consulting and public-relations firm, and co-founded the IT company Structura. Between 2013 and 2020, companies affiliated with him won Russian government contracts worth about $2.7 million for website maintenance, polling, advertising, and focus groups. One firm, Deep Communication, worked on a state-funded 2016 film. His other ventures have included restaurants, insurance, a reading-aloud championship, and a Bulgarian company that advertised Black Sea and Greek real estate and never made a profit. He does not regard these as unrelated interests. He regards them as different rooms in the same building.

That building is the Institute. The logic is less strange than it first appears. Every campaign he has worked on began with a base: a person, a district, a set of assumptions that had to be made comfortable before anything could be asked of them. Boxer shorts perform the same function in a life. They are the first layer, the least visible, the most frequently underestimated. A suit can be borrowed. A message can be tested. The first layer must be chosen.

He founded Ilya Boxers Institute to give that layer a standard, a vocabulary, and a place to keep its findings. IBI does not claim to have invented the boxer short. It claims jurisdiction over the question of what the boxer short is for. The Institute treats underwear as infrastructure: the garment that negotiates with the day before the day begins, the private agreement beneath public appearance. Most public life is conducted above the waist. It is supported below it. That is not a joke. It is a design brief.

The Institute therefore applies the methods of campaign thinking to a subject that has no constituency and every wearer. It studies the base rather than the podium. It prefers a durable arrangement to a memorable gesture. It measures success by the absence of complaint. The founder's career has been varied, but its through-line is simple: he has always worked on the layer people stand on before they speak. Ilya Boxers Institute is that layer, given a department of its own.

## The Ilya Method

### 1. Begin with the base.

Political campaigns fail at the door, the stairwell, and the polling station — the places where the argument meets a life. Underwear meets the same test at the waistband, the seam, and the transition from sitting to standing. Design for the body at rest, not the body in the photograph.

### 2. Make the invisible structural.

A message is rarely defeated by its opposite; it is defeated by friction. The Institute treats the hidden points of contact as the real site of design. If the first layer is wrong, every layer above it is a cover story.

### 3. One message, many rooms.

A national campaign succeeds when a single idea can be repeated in different accents. A pair of boxer shorts must work under a suit, in a heatwave, on a night train, and in a hospital corridor. The Institute designs for rooms, not mirrors.

### 4. Field-test the ordinary.

The useful evidence is gathered in ordinary conditions: a long day, a warm room, a meeting that runs late. The Institute does not confuse a laboratory with a life. A garment that works only in a fitting room has been flattered, not tested.

### 5. Leave the wearer alone.

The most persuasive object is the one that does not demand gratitude. Good underwear should recede into the day, not request a review. The Institute measures success by the absence of complaint.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why boxers?

Because boxers are the first layer of public life. A tie is a statement; a boxer short is a condition. The Institute prefers to work on conditions.

### Why an institute?

Because standards need a place to live and a memory to survive. A label can sell a garment; an institute can decide what the garment is for. IBI exists to keep that decision honest.

### Why IBI?

The Institute began as IBI International Burke Institute, a geopolitics research institute. It is now the Ilya Boxers Institute. The initials were retained because institutions should not change their initials every time they change their minds.

### What does “field-tested” mean?

At IBI, “field-tested” is a standard of evidence, not a laboratory boast. It means the first layer has been judged in the conditions in which it is actually worn: under a suit, on a long journey, in a room whose temperature cannot be controlled. A garment that works only in a fitting room has been flattered, not tested.

### What is the sizing philosophy?

Sizing is a form of governance. It should be legible, consistent, and resistant to flattery. The Institute does not believe a waistband should depend on guesswork, or that self-esteem should depend on a label.

### What is the index?

The IBI Index is the Institute's periodic editorial reading of the first layer. It considers coverage, recovery, waistband discipline, and the dignity of the ordinary. It ranks conditions, not people, and it is not a forecast.
